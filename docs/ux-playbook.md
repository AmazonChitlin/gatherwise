# EventLocal UX Playbook

EventLocal helps vendors, food trucks, artists, market organizers, small event hosts, venues, and pop-up businesses get clearer about local event readiness before they set up.

Brand:

- Name: EventLocal
- Tagline: Ready. Set. Local.
- Core idea: Get your local event ready before you set up.
- Emotional promise: Less guessing. Fewer surprises. A clearer path before event day.

## UX Principles

- Make the first screen obvious. A visitor should understand what EventLocal does, who it is for, and what to do next without hunting.
- Design for scanning, not reading. Use short headings, plain labels, cards, badges, and grouped sections.
- Use recognition over recall. Offer practical choices and examples instead of asking users to know agency terms.
- Start with words. Good UX starts with clear copy before layout decoration.
- Respect user anxiety. Assume users may be worried about permits, deadlines, fees, or getting turned away.
- Make uncertainty visible. Show confidence, verification status, source links, and “confirm with agency” language.
- Keep disclaimers visible but not overwhelming. Disclaimers should be easy to find and written plainly.
- Use ethical persuasion only. Encourage readiness and planning, not fear.
- Avoid legal certainty. Do not imply EventLocal has decided what is legally required.
- Avoid government jargon unless explained. When a term is necessary, translate it into plain English.

## Tone

EventLocal should sound:

- Plain English
- Practical
- Local
- Calm
- Honest
- Slightly warm
- No hype
- No legal conclusions

Preferred wording:

- “Likely required”
- “May be required”
- “Confirm with the agency”
- “Check the official source”
- “Informational guidance only, not legal advice”

Avoid:

- “You are compliant”
- “This permit is required” unless a verified rule record explicitly supports that wording
- “Guaranteed”
- “Approved”
- “We handle the permit for you”
- Fear-based copy

## Visual Direction

EventLocal's current visual theme is `EventLocal Command`: a premium dark/white/orange product-site direction with a deep navy hero, bright orange CTAs, white content sections, clean dashboard preview cards, charcoal text, and green/blue-green verification accents.

EventLocal should feel like a polished readiness product, not a government portal, legal office, childish illustration system, or generic beige/desert theme.

Useful patterns:

- Cards for grouped information.
- Badges for requirement level, confidence, and verification status.
- Timeline blocks for lead time and planning windows.
- Source links that look official and easy to inspect.
- Checklist patterns for next steps.
- Calm colors, good spacing, and readable typography.

The visual system should support trust, clarity, and action. It should not look like a checkout funnel or a legal warning wall.

## Homepage Guidance

The homepage should quickly answer:

- What is EventLocal?
- Who is it for?
- What problem does it solve?
- What should I do first?

Primary message:

> Get your local event ready before you set up.

Supporting message should mention vendors, organizers, food sellers, performers, and venue hosts, while staying concise.

The main CTA should lead to intake. Secondary links can point to the disclaimer or about page.

## Intake Form Guidance

The intake form should feel practical, not like paperwork.

Use:

- Clear sections.
- Short question labels.
- Familiar words first.
- Examples when helpful.
- Optional detail fields grouped under food, alcohol, fire/temporary structures, traffic, signage/promotion, and property.

Avoid:

- Asking users to know permit names.
- Long agency-specific explanations in the form.
- Legal conclusions.
- Making every edge-case field feel equally urgent.

The form should make users feel like they are describing their setup, not testifying to a government agency.

## Results Page Guidance

The results page should make the next review path clear without overstating certainty.

Results should show:

- Event summary.
- Basic checklist.
- City, county, and state guidance together when rules stack.
- Timeline based on lead time.
- Red flags.
- Agency contacts.
- Official source links.
- Verification labels.
- Visible disclaimer.

No-match results should be honest:

- Do not imply nothing applies.
- Explain that current rule records did not match.
- Encourage confirming with the relevant city, county, or state agency.

Many-match results should remain scannable through grouping, badges, and clear source links.

## Disclaimers And Trust Language

Disclaimers should be visible on intake, results, and about/disclaimer surfaces.

Required concepts:

- EventLocal provides informational guidance only.
- EventLocal is not legal advice.
- EventLocal does not submit permits.
- EventLocal does not guarantee compliance.
- Requirements can change.
- Users should confirm with the relevant agency.

Trust comes from showing uncertainty clearly, not hiding it.

## Microinteractions

Microinteractions should reduce uncertainty and support task completion.

Useful examples:

- Clear loading state while saving intake.
- Readable validation errors near the relevant field.
- Disabled future CTAs that plainly say the feature is not active.
- Link styling that makes official sources recognizable.
- Badges that make confidence and verification status visible.

Avoid playful motion or gamification around legal/permit uncertainty. Subtle feedback is enough.

## Future Paid Roadmap Messaging

Future paid roadmap and vendor packet messaging should stay transparent.

Say:

- “Future paid feature, not active in this MVP.”
- “EventLocal does not process payments or generate downloads yet.”
- “A future roadmap could include your checklist, timeline, agency contacts, document prep list, red flags, and next steps.”

Do not say or imply:

- The user can buy now.
- A PDF will be generated now.
- Payment is available.
- The roadmap guarantees compliance.

## Usability Testing With Vendors

Test with real or realistic users:

- Food truck owner.
- Retail pop-up vendor.
- Artist or performer.
- Market organizer.
- Venue or property host.
- Small community event organizer.

Useful prompts:

- “What do you think this site does?”
- “What would you click first?”
- “Which question feels confusing?”
- “Do these results feel useful or overwhelming?”
- “Would you know who to call next?”
- “Does any wording sound too certain?”
- “Would you trust this more or less if a source link is visible?”

Watch for:

- Users missing the disclaimer.
- Users assuming EventLocal submits permits.
- Users confusing sample/future paid features with active services.
- Users skipping source links.
- Users not understanding city/county/state stacking.
- Users feeling overwhelmed by refined intake fields.

The goal of testing is not to prove the interface is clever. The goal is to find where a local vendor or organizer still has to guess.
